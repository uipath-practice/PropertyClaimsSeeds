## Recommended Scope

**Recommendation:** SOLUTION(Case Management, Agents, API Workflows, Data Fabric entity, Coded Action App)
**Delivery model:** cloud
**Blocked by platform:** none
**Need profile:** Settle a household property claim end to end in ≤ 2 business days when nothing is wrong with it, and put exactly the claims that need judgement in front of exactly one human — SC1 ≥ 30% straight-through with SC5 ≤ 10% false escalation.
**Reasoning:**

- A claim lives for days to weeks, changes hands, and must be readable in flight (§5.4 *State outlives any single step*) → **Case Management**
- Five of the twelve automated determinations are marked *judgement* over free prose — a policy, an assessor report, a claimant's account (§5.3, §5.6) → **Agents**
- The other seven are marked *rule-expressible* and must give the same answer twice on the same claim (§1.3 SC6, CONFIG model settings) → **API Workflows**
- Every step's output has to be readable by later steps and by a human while the claim is open (§1.5 P5) → **Data Fabric entity**
- Two human decisions need every finding side by side, not a summary (§5.7 H1, H2) → **Coded Action App**
- Claim form reading is a fixed-layout extraction already running (§5.6, Appendix D2) → **adopt the provided IXP model**

**Alternatives considered:**

- **Maestro Flow instead of a case** — rejected: a flow has no stage lifecycle, no stage-level SLA and no in-flight case view, and §5.5 asks for all three.
- **One analysis component covering coverage, settlement and credibility** — rejected: §7's *one concern, one owner* rule and §5.4's three-way parallel fork both require them separate, and a single component reporting three concerns produces one caution that reads as three problems.
- **A settlement authorisation / payment service at 6.2** — rejected: OS2 puts payment outside this process entirely; 6.2 records an amount and an approver and stops.
- **A central record-writing component** — rejected: the claim record is a store the case writes to, not a service to call; routing every write through one component invents a dependency that does not exist.
- **Splitting BR-05 into a deterministic day-count plus a judgement tail** — rejected: it puts one rule under two owners, which is exactly the failure §7's preamble names.

## Section 1: Case Definition

### Case Metadata

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Case Name | ClaimCase-V2A |
| Case Description | Handles one household property damage claim from the moment it is filed to the moment the claimant is told the outcome. It reads the claim form, the policy and the independent assessor report, screens the claim before an inspection is paid for, decides what the policy covers and what it pays, and puts a human in front of the two decisions that need one. A claim with nothing wrong with it settles without any human being asked to look. |
| Case Identifier | Type: constant. Constant → Prefix: `CLM` |
| Priority | Choiceset: Urgent, High, Medium, Low — Default: Medium |
| Case-Level SLA | 8 d |
| SLA Title | Claim decision deadline |
| SLA Type | time-based |
| Case App | Enabled |
| Task-output passing | Direct |
| Case Identifier source | `=metadata.ExternalId` |

**Why the case mints the claim reference.** §6.2 makes *Claim reference* the identifier the claim carries everywhere it appears, and §5.3 step 1.1 creates it. The platform already mints exactly one such value per instance and exposes it as `metadata.ExternalId`, and the claim-generation automation takes a claim number as an input so the number printed on the three documents equals it. Minting it anywhere else gives the claim two identifiers and lets the one nobody intended win.

**Every duration in this document is business days** — Monday to Friday, excluding public holidays in the country the policy was issued in (§5.5, §12 V1). A stage SLA is a plain duration with no calendar field, so the count is authored here and the business calendar is attached at build time. BR-05's 60-day filing deadline is **not** an SLA and is deliberately calendar days; it is a rule applied to dates by task 2.5 and never a platform deadline.

### Case-Level SLA Escalation Rules

**Design Rationale:** §1.2 targets 8 business days from receipt to decision letter for all claims and §5.5 sets the whole-claim SLA to match, with at-risk at 80%. Both thresholds notify only — §5.5 is explicit that **a breach never closes or refuses a claim**, so neither row cancels, decides or reroutes anything, and no secondary stage is entered on an SLA status change. Notification means the claim appears in the dashboard view the Claims team lead is already watching (RP4); no message is sent to an individual and Correspondence is never used for an internal alert.

| SLA Status | Threshold | Action | Display Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| At-Risk | 80% of SLA duration | Notify: Role: Claims team lead | Claim deadline at risk |
| Breached | 100% of SLA duration | Notify: Role: Claims team lead, Role: Claims Operations Manager | Claim deadline breached |

### Case Exit Conditions

| WHEN | IF | THEN | Marks Case Complete | Display Name |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| required-stages-completed | — | Case exited — E1 Settled | Yes | Claim settled |
| selected-stage-completed("Denied") | — | Case exited — E2 or E3 Refused | No | Claim refused |

**Both endings, and only these two.** §5.5 lists four exits. E1 *Settled* and E4 *Settled at zero* are the same exit — a claim whose covered total is absorbed by the deductible is a valid covered claim that pays nothing (BR-26), so it completes through `required-stages-completed` exactly like any other approval and is never modelled as a refusal. E2 *Refused at screening* and E3 *Refused at review* are the same exit too: both reach the `Denied` lane, from mutually exclusive origins, and a claim takes exactly one of the two.

**Why so many rows, when most carry one value to one consumer.** `contracts/claim-entity.md` pins one name across three surfaces — what a component returns, the case variable, and the column on the claim record — and says not to improve any of the three. Every row above whose name matches a pinned column exists to keep that name intact through the case; collapsing it into a direct task-to-task reference would leave the record write with nothing to bind by name.

**Every default is written as a string, for every type**, because a non-string default is dropped on serialization and leaves the variable null at run time for its first reader. The defaults are also what makes the case safe at t=0: every condition in this design is evaluated once at case start, before any task has run, so no condition parses a JSON payload and every one compares a value that already exists.

## Section 2: Stages & Tasks

**How to read the I/O tables.** An input bound in the `vars` namespace reads a declared case variable. An input bound `"Stage"."Task".out_Field` reads an upstream task's output directly, without a relaying case variable — used wherever exactly one downstream component consumes a payload. An output row bound `—` is produced by the component and read downstream by that same direct reference; it is deliberately not relayed through a case variable.

**Every generated component returns the same three outputs.** `out_Findings` is the finding envelope specified in the [Data Model](#the-finding-envelope--the-shared-component-contract), declared as a **typed object** rather than a JSON string; `out_Conclusion` is the component's own one-word conclusion; `out_FlagSubjects` is a comma-separated list of the escalation subjects that component raised, or the literal `none`. Components that also produce business data return exactly one further payload, named for the record column it lands in. This holds for the agents and for the deterministic runners alike — one reviewer sees every finding at once, and eight differently-shaped payloads is a screen nobody can build.

**A `*ChecksJson` column is written by the deterministic component that consumes the envelope**, never straight from an agent output: task 2.6 writes `eligibilityChecksJson`, task 4.3 writes its own `payoutChecksJson`, and task 5.1 writes `assessmentReportValidationJson`, `coverageChecksJson` and `credibilityChecksJson`. That component is also where the column's character budget is enforced, and it runs before the gate that reads it, so C3 still holds.

**Two limits govern every payload in this design, and they are different limits.** The first is the record's: a column holds 10,000 characters and its producer budgets to 8,000. The second is the consumer's: **the arguments a component is started with are capped, and the cap is on all of them together.** Three payloads that each fit their own column will not fit one component's input, so every consumer carries an input budget and every producer is told the smallest budget any of its consumers imposes.

### Stage 1: Intake (`stage-intake`)

**Type:** Stage
**Stage Kind:** primary
**Design Rationale:** Everything a screening check needs has to exist before screening starts, and one thing decides the shape of this stage: the policy is fetched **by policy number**, and the policy number is on the claim form. So the claim and its documents come first, then the form is read, and only then can the policy and the claim history be fetched — those two in parallel, along with the receipt to the claimant, which needs only the contact details the form gave up. §5.5 gives Intake no deadline of its own, so this stage carries no SLA; the whole-claim SLA is already running.
**Description:** Registers the claim, assembles its three documents, reads the claim form into structured claim data, fetches the policy and the claim history for the period, reads the policy into structured policy data, and tells the claimant the claim was received.
**Required for Case Completion:** Yes

**Design Rationale:** This is the check that sits before an inspection is paid for, which is the whole reason it exists as its own stage rather than as part of Analysis. Both human tasks are therefore addressed to a role rather than to a named person, which is what makes them delegable and what stops a claim stalling behind one person's absence.

## Data Model

### The finding envelope — the shared component contract

Every check and analysis in this design returns the same payload shape. This is a design decision rather than a requirement: §5.7 H2 puts every finding from stage 4 side by side on one screen, §7 requires one concern to have exactly one owner, and BR-41 requires an escalation condition to be met **by its own subject**. None of those three is enforceable if each component invents its own vocabulary, and a screen cannot render eight different shapes.

**Three outputs, on every component:**

| Output | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `out_Findings` | **Object**, budgeted to 8,000 characters serialised | The envelope below, declared field by field in the component's own output schema |
| `out_Conclusion` | String | The component's own one-word conclusion, from its own closed list |
| `out_FlagSubjects` | String | Comma-separated escalation subjects this component raised, or the literal `none` |

> **`[MEASURED]` The envelope is a typed object because a hand-serialised string does not survive contact with a real claim.** The first build of these components declared the envelope as a JSON string and stated its shape in prose, with two integer counts the component had to make agree with its own payload. On the first five live runs that produced two distinct defects: one component returned a finding count of 5 beside an envelope holding 6 findings, and one destroyed its whole payload by writing `"totalClaimed":64500.0` into an evidence quote without escaping the inner quotation marks — invalid JSON, and a claim that would have faulted at the record write. Redeclaring the envelope as a typed object fixed both at once: the runtime does the serialising, so quoting cannot break it, and there is no free-text count left to disagree with anything. **Never ask a component to serialise its own payload when the schema can declare it.**
>
> Two rules survive from that first build because they earned it. **No text value may contain a double-quote character** — quote with single quotes or none — which keeps every payload safe wherever it is later embedded. And **the set is never trimmed to fit a budget**: prose is shortened with an ellipsis, and a finding, an exclusion, a limit or a named peril is never dropped, because a missing exclusion reads downstream as an exclusion the policy does not have.

**The envelope:**

| Field | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `schema` | string | `claim-finding-envelope/1` — the version the reader parses against |
| `component` | string | The component that produced it |
| `step` | string | The PDD step it implements, e.g. `4.2` |
| `claimId` | string | The claim reference |
| `conclusion` | string | The same word as `out_Conclusion` |
| `confidence` | string | `high`, `medium` or `low` |
| `findings` | array | One entry per rule the component owns — **always present, passes included** |
| `context` | array | Things a reader may want to know that are not findings |
| `analysis` | object | The component's own structured result, or `{}` |
| `humanContext` | object or null | What a human already decided and why, where one has |
| `unreadable` | array | Named inputs the component could not read. **Never empty on a silence it noticed** |

**A `findings` entry:**

| Field | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `ruleId` | string | The `PDD.md` §7 rule id — `BR-02`, `BR-15`. Every finding has exactly one |
| `check` | string | The check's own name, stable across claims, e.g. `Identity` |
| `result` | string | `pass` · `fail` · `caveat` · `low` · `medium` · `high` · `proceed` · `escalate` · `unusable` · `not-applicable` |
| `flag` | boolean | Whether this counts as a flag for BR-44 and BR-45 |
| `escalationSubject` | string | `eligibility` · `report` · `coverage` · `settlement` · `credibility` · `aggregate` · `none` — **this component's own subject, never a neighbour's** |
| `headline` | string | One sentence a reviewer can act on, ≤ 140 characters |
| `reason` | string | Why this result, in the reviewer's language |
| `evidence` | array | `{ source, quote }` — the exact words relied on, and which of the four documents they came from |
| `cites` | array | Rule ids whose findings this one used as evidence and is **not** re-raising |

**A `context` entry:** `{ ruleId, note, whyNotAFinding }`. It carries the §7.9 test's answer in the third field, in words, so a reader can see the judgement rather than infer it.

### How a finding is worded

§7.9 says what is *not* a finding, and it decides whether anything can settle automatically at all. These ten rules are the contract, and they are built as carefully as the rules that say what to look for.

| # | Rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| W1 | **The test, every time: could this change what is paid, to whom, or whether the loss is covered?** If it could not, it is a `context` entry with the answer written into `whyNotAFinding` — never a `findings` entry with a non-pass result. | §7.9's opening. A difference that cannot change the outcome is context, and a reader who wants it will find it in the summary. |
| W2 | **A pass is written as an affirmative statement with its values quoted**, not as silence and not as a hedge. `Policy status Paid in full; period 2025-06-01 to 2026-05-31; in force on the loss date 2026-02-14.` | All five screening checks are reported whatever the result, and a reviewer's screen is built from what was recorded (C3). A blank pass is indistinguishable from a check that never ran. |
| W3 | **A fail states the fact and quotes both sides where two values differ.** `Address fails — the claim gives #12-456 and the policy gives #12-465; street, city and postcode agree.` | BR-03, BR-04, BR-08, BR-15, BR-29 and BR-30 all require both values. A reviewer who has to go and find the other number will stop reading the caveats. |
| W4 | **No hedging verbs.** `may`, `might`, `possibly`, `appears to`, `potentially`, `it is unclear whether` are banned in `headline` and `reason`. A component that cannot decide records `unusable` and flags it; it does not write a soft fail. | *An absent answer is not a pass* — and a hedged finding is an absent answer wearing a finding's clothes. It reaches a reviewer as a worry with nothing to act on. |
| W5 | **A component never re-raises another's finding.** Where it needs one, it names it in `cites` and words the sentence as borrowed: *"as BR-29 found, the claimed total is 38% above the assessor's"* — never *"the claimed total is too high"*. | §7's preamble. One problem reported by three components reads on a reviewer's screen as three unrelated problems. |
| W6 | **`escalationSubject` is the component's own subject.** Settlement raises `settlement`, never `coverage`; credibility raises `credibility`, never `settlement`. | BR-41's conditions are met by their own subject, not by any concern anywhere. Reading a settlement caution as *"coverage is ambiguous"* is what turns every claim into an escalation and makes SC1 unreachable. |
| W7 | **Silence is not evidence.** Where the documents do not discuss a condition — occupancy, vacancy, wear, defect, neglect — the component records nothing at all, not a `context` note asking someone to confirm it. | BR-72 and BR-78. Asking a reviewer to confirm four things nobody ever recorded is how they learn to stop reading the caveats. |
| W8 | **Different words are not a discrepancy.** Two professionals describing the same damage differently, a remembered time that moved, a field the issuing country does not use — none of these is written as a finding. | BR-71 and BR-32. *An address with no state is a Singapore address, not an incomplete one.* |
| W9 | **Materiality is not leniency.** A real problem is never recorded inside a check that passes. BR-15 is the counter-example: a peril conflict **fails** even when the assessor's peril is itself covered. | BR-77. Recording a real problem inside a passing check is how it reaches approval with a note nobody reads. |
| W10 | **A clean claim returns findings with every `result` a pass, `flag` false everywhere, `escalationSubject` `none` everywhere, `context` and `unreadable` empty, and `out_FlagSubjects` the literal `none`.** That is a complete, correct answer and not a silence. | Roughly a third of claims carry nothing wrong. A build that finds something on every claim has learned to always answer *yes* to *"is anything wrong here?"* — the easiest way to look thorough and the least useful. This is what SC5 measures. |

**Four things about this table are load-bearing.**

**The two gates do not share columns.** Screening writes `eligibility*` and review writes `review*`. Sharing them lets the second gate destroy the first gate's record, and the eligibility reviewer's reason is the one thing that stops a granted exception being re-raised as a breach.

**4,000 characters, not 200, on both note fields.** These are a reviewer's own words and the only place their reasoning survives; 200 cuts a paragraph mid-sentence and tells nobody it did.

**Every JSON column is capped at 10,000 characters and every producer budgets to 8,000.** The cap is not silent — an over-length write faults the whole claim — so each component that produces a payload carries its own budget rather than a hope, and 8,000 leaves room for a claim with more damage rows than usual. Where a payload would exceed it, the component drops its lowest-value detail first: raw quotes are truncated with an ellipsis before any finding is dropped, and a finding is never dropped.

**The write is a patch, not a replace, and its three cases are not symmetric.** An omitted field keeps its content; `null` destroys it; and an empty string destroys it too, silently, while reporting success. An unset case variable resolves to an empty string, which is the worst of the three. So **every optional field is coalesced to omitted rather than to blank** at every write point in this design — no task writes a field it did not produce on this run, and no field is ever set from a variable that might still be at its default.

| Claim submission form | Filed by the claimant through Claims Intake | Bucket `Claims`, as `<claimId>-claim.pdf` | IXP model property-claims-shared-45fcad56-ixp, then task 1.3 | 7 years from closure, enforced outside this process |
| Insurance policy | Retrieved from Policy Administration by policy number | Bucket `Insurance Policies Repository`, as `<policyId>.pdf` | Agent — task 1.6 reads it, task 4.2 quotes it | 7 years from closure, enforced outside this process |
| Assessor report | Filed against the claim by the independent assessor | Bucket `Assessor Reports`, as `<claimId>-incident-report.pdf` | Agent — task 4.1 | 7 years from closure, enforced outside this process |
| Claim history for the policy period | Returned by Claims History | Bucket `Claims`, as `<policyId>-history.json` | None — read as data by tasks 4.3 and 4.4 | 7 years from closure, enforced outside this process |
| Decision letter | Written inside the ending | `claimResponseJson` on the claim record, and the job log | None | 7 years from closure, enforced outside this process |

> **Nothing in this design reads `<claimId>-claim.json`.** It sits in the `Claims` bucket beside the claim forms and it states which problems were planted and what the outcome should be. It is the test oracle. A component that consulted it would be brilliant and worthless.

### Additional Entities

> None. One claim is one row; the settlement, the findings and the decisions are payloads on that row rather than child tables, because every one of them is read as a whole by the screen or the report that needs it and none is queried across claims.

### Choice Sets

> None. `status`, `eligibilityDecision`, `reviewDecision` and `claimOutcome` are strings rather than choice sets. Choice-set values are stored as integer ids assigned by creation order, and a report or a screen that has to resolve four id maps to show four words is harder to read and easier to get wrong than the words themselves.

### Virtual / External Data

> None.

## Non-Functional Requirements

| Dimension | Requirement / design decision |
|---|---|
| Security | Credentials and connection identities live in Orchestrator assets and the shared Data Fabric connection; no secret appears in this design, in a component's prompt, or in a package. The reviewer screens sign in through the shared external application with read-only, user-scoped permissions — the screen reads, the case writes. Claimant contact details and the identity reference are masked in every view outside the claims team. |
| Performance | A claim with nothing wrong reaches a decision letter in ≤ 2 business days (SC2) and all claims average ≤ 8 (SC3). Within a stage, no automated task holds the claim for more than the stage SLA: eligibility screening and analysis are one business day each. The assessor wait is excluded from automation performance — it is a third party's clock. |
| Scalability | 2,400 claims a year, 180–260 a month, peaking at 340 in storm season. Claims are handled **per item** — one claim at a time, one claim failing affects no other — so the ceiling is concurrent case instances rather than batch size. The three-way parallel fork in Analysis is the only place one claim runs three components at once. |
| Availability / Resilience | A source system that is unavailable is retried, then the claim is **parked and made visible** — never failed (E-01). A document that cannot be read is parked and referred to a human, and is **never treated as an empty one** (E-02). Correspondence that cannot be sent is retried then parked, and **the decision stands** — the claim is decided, only the telling failed (E-03). A parked claim stays in its stage, where the stage SLA already makes it visible; nothing before the ending is irreversible, so parking is safe. |
| Logging & Monitoring | Every task records what it produced on the claim record. The claims team lead watches the in-flight view (RP1, RP2, RP4); the Claims Operations Manager watches closed-today (RP3) and the straight-through rate (RP5). Every SLA notification lands on that dashboard and nowhere else — no message is sent to an individual and Correspondence is never used for an internal alert. |
| Compliance | C1 — no claim is refused without a named human decision-maker and a written reason; a Deny recommendation always opens the claim-review gate, with no exception. C2 — approval within tolerance runs unattended, and the automatic decision is recorded **as a decision**. C3 — every payload a reviewer is meant to see is written before their gate opens. C4 — the claimant is told the outcome and the reason, and the letter is stored against the claim. C5 — the annual aggregate always reaches a human, with a reason naming the earlier claim. C6 — claimant contact details, the identity reference and the full claim narrative are never written to a log or a trace. |
| Data residency & retention | A claim is handled in the country the policy was issued in; residency here means **access control, not hosting** — all six countries are served from one region under one corporate licence, and a claim is visible only to the claims team of its own country. Records and documents are retained 7 years from closure. **Nothing in this process deletes anything**; the periods are enforced by an archival process outside it and owned by the Claims Operations Manager. |

### UAT Acceptance Criteria

| # | Criterion | Target | Pass / Fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| SC1 | Straight-through processing rate — claims reaching a decision letter with no human touch at any point | ≥ 30% | |
| SC2 | Cycle time, straight-through claim | ≤ 2 business days | |
| SC3 | Cycle time, all claims | ≤ 8 business days average | |
| SC4 | Detection accuracy — claims carrying a known material problem stopped at the correct decision point, with the problem named | 100% | |
| SC5 | False escalation rate — claims with nothing materially wrong nevertheless referred to a human | ≤ 10% | |
| SC6 | Decision traceability — decisions recording who decided, when, what they were shown, and the written reason | 100% | |

> **SC4 and SC5 pull against each other, and that tension is the measure of this design.** Stopping every claim satisfies SC4 and fails SC5 and SC1. Never stopping one does the reverse.

### Test Data & Cleanup

**Source:** Claims are generated on demand by the deployed claim-generation automation, complete with their three documents, aimed at any rule or exception path in §13.2 through `scenario` and `discrepancy`, or produced clean.
**Reset between runs:** Test claims and their records are removed with the environment they were created in. Each run uses a fresh case instance and therefore a fresh claim reference, so no run overwrites another's documents. `<claimId>-claim.json` is read **only** by the verification step, never by anything this solution builds.

## Design Feedback to PDD

| # | PDD section / step | What the design changed | Reason | Impact class |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | §5.3 steps 1.1 and 1.2, §5.4 | The parallel fork between opening the record and collecting the documents is collapsed into one task | One deployed automation performs both in a single run, and calling it twice would regenerate the claimant's documents mid-case. Nothing observable changes: the record exists and all three documents are assembled before anything reads them | Technology-driven |
| 2 | §5.3 step 1.4 | A second task reads the policy document into structured policy data | §5.3 describes business activity, not components, and gives step 1.4 only *the policy document is attached*. Three screening checks and the whole of coverage need the policy's terms as data, and two of those checks are rule-expressible and cannot read prose. The read is what makes step 1.4's document usable | Technology-driven |
| 3 | §5.3 step 1.3 | A second task flattens the extraction payload into plain values | The extraction result is deeply nested and carries per-field confidence; a case expression resolves one level, and the payload would not fit the record's 10,000-character column with its confidences attached | Technology-driven |
| 4 | §5.4 and §7.4 together, steps 4.2 and 4.3 | The settlement is computed blind to the coverage verdict, and the two are joined at step 5.1 | §5.4 requires 4.2 ∥ 4.3 with **neither reading the other's output**, while BR-20 requires a coverage verdict per line. The only step that may legally hold both is 5.1, which §5.3 already defines as reading every finding from stages 2 and 4. Task 4.3 still owns and reports BR-20 to BR-29; task 5.1 raises no settlement finding of its own and cites BR-20 and BR-25 as the rules it applied. **This is the one place where a business reader should check the design against their intent** | Business-process-driven — the answer is unchanged, but which step produces the final net payable moves |
| 5 | §5.3 step 3.1, §9 | The wait for the assessor report is a short poll rather than a subscription | §9 names *Assessor report available* as an inbound event, but the deployed retrieval automation answers readiness on demand and no connector publishes the event. The business behaviour is identical — the claim waits and the stage SLA runs — and the poll interval carries no business meaning | Technology-driven |
| 6 | §5.3 step 6.2 | No settlement, payment or authorisation call exists | OS2 and N2 put paying outside this process and §9 records that Settlements receives an amount and an approver and nothing else. The task records the authorised amount and who authorised it and stops | Technology-driven |
| 7 | §5.3 step 8.1, §15 | The Missing details lane is modelled complete and inert behind a guard that defaults false and that nothing produces | §15 says the stage must exist in the lifecycle so the later change is an addition rather than a restructure, and must not be built. A stage with no entry condition would be orphaned rather than deferred | Technology-driven |
| 8 | §5.6, §12 V1 | The policy reader converts the policy's printed dates to ISO and carries the printed text alongside | **`[MEASURED]`** §12 says the date format varies by issuing country and says nothing more. On a live claim the policy printed `11/08/2025`, the reader refused to guess whether that was 11 August or 8 November — correctly, under the rule that forbids inventing a value — and reported the policy period **unreadable** on a claim with nothing wrong with it. All six countries in §12 V1 write day-first; the design now states that, and requires the printed text to be carried so the conversion stays auditable. A caution raised on a clean claim is exactly what SC5 counts | Technology-driven |
| 9 | §6.4, §1.5 P5 | Every component carries an input budget as well as a payload budget | **`[MEASURED]`** The record's columns hold 10,000 characters each, and the design passes three of them into one component. The arguments a component is started with are capped, and the cap is on all of them together — a live run was refused at roughly 8,700 characters of arguments against a documented 10,000, the difference being the platform's own envelope. Payload budgets alone do not prevent this, so each producer is now told the smallest budget any of its consumers imposes. **This is the first thing to verify when the case is wired** | Technology-driven |
| 10 | §6.2 Object: Claim | The claim reference is the case's own identifier rather than one assigned by Claims Intake | The claim-generation automation stamps the number this process supplies onto all three documents, so one value identifies the case, the documents and the record. Under a live Claims Intake integration the inbound reference would become the identifier's external source instead — a one-line change to the case identifier, not a restructure | Technology-driven |
